# Adversarial check of the six evidence reports

Written 2026-11-06 for the user lab. Each claim below would change what the lab does: a behaviour for simulated
people, a verdict that changes or removes an instruction in `behaviour-guide.md` section 5, or a figure for how good
experts or model stand-ins are at finding problems. For each claim I tried to refute it. I reopened the source's own
text, usually through the filed copy in `research/library/store/<id>.txt`. I checked that the figure, the population
and the effect size match the report. I looked for a later study or critique the report missed, and I checked that
the claim does not reach beyond what was measured.

**Verdicts.** *Confirmed*: the source says it, on the population stated. *Narrower*: the figure is right, but it holds
for less than the report says, or it is weaker than reported; the column says how. *Wrong*: the source says
something else; the column says what is right. *Unsupported*: the conclusion drawn does not follow from what was
measured.

Every report correction is marked in place with "*Check 2026-11-06 (`2026-11-05-check.md`):*". Two sources here
were not opened in full and are marked: Josef et al. 2016 (abstract and press release only, logged with
`library.mjs tried`) and Hervet et al. 2011 (abstract on IDEAS, read with WebFetch; the page's bot check stopped
`library.mjs add`).

**Counts.** 66 claims checked: 44 confirmed, 16 narrower, 4 wrong, 2 unsupported. 40 places in the six reports were
corrected or annotated.

## The claims that changed

1. **Model twins and the default effect** (AI stand-ins), *wrong*. The report says the twins "reproduced a classic
   published default effect but not the same effect in a new design". The paper's table of substudies (p. 44) says
   something else. In the classic organ-donation design, the study's own people showed **no** default effect, and the
   twins showed a strong one. In the new green-energy design, the people showed a default effect and the twins showed
   none. The twins matched the textbook against the people, so the lesson "research does not belong in the reviewer as
   rules" is stronger than the report said.
2. **"One expert finds about a third of users' problems (30 to 43%)"** (expert review), *wrong*. These five figures
   come from **pooled groups** of evaluators: four inspectors (Sauro 2012), groups of 10 (Hvannberg et al. 2007) and
   18 novices (Law and Hvannberg 2004). In Hvannberg et al., one evaluator predicted 6.6 problems on average. Of
   these, 2.6 were seen in the users' test, against 58 problems that the users met in all, about 4% each. A single
   reviewer is therefore further below the 70% bar than the report said.
3. **The Israeli text-message nudge** (replication), *wrong in size*. The report has "about 15%, less than half for
   Haredi and Arab parents", from a press release it did not open. The paper itself gives +12 percentage points of
   active choice for the general population (OLS, Table 1), and says the effect "is halved" for Arab and
   Ultra-Orthodox parents.
4. **Fees as a ten-year money cost** (numbers report, persona item 4), *wrong*. The item paired the 12% of
   experiment 1's control group with the 13.1% of an experiment 2 cell, and concluded that money framing barely
   helps. In the same cells of experiment 2a, the share choosing the cheapest fund doubled from 6.3% to 13.1%. The
   share choosing the top past-return fund fell from 56.8% to 36.9% (2b, investors: 67.9% to 38.5%). A large multi-year
   shekel sum does move people, which fits Newall and Love's "peanuts" result.
5. **SUTM's checker "cut self-contradictions from 45% to 0.8%"** (AI stand-ins), *unsupported* as "the technique
   with the largest measured effect". The 0.8% belongs to a whole pipeline (persona packs, supervisor checks and
   re-running unstable segments), not to a checker alone. The baseline reports the same 45% for two different measures.
   The paper does not say how contradictions were coded, by whom, or over how many runs. The prototypes were not car
   dashboards: they were common login, checkout, booking and account-recovery patterns. Try the check; do not count
   on its size.
6. **Second-language readers "drop whole dense blocks"** (phone, age and language), *unsupported* for this group. The
   measured part is that they read slower and skip fewer words. That was measured on Dutch students reading a novel in
   English and on Japanese classroom learners of English. Dropping whole blocks was observed in NN/g's lower-literacy
   users, who were native readers.
7. **"Older is not more cautious"** (phone, age and language), *narrower*. Mata et al. covered lab gambles with
   stated odds. In Germany's household panel (44,076 adults), people's stated willingness to take risks, money
   included, falls with age, most after about 65 (Josef et al. 2016, abstract only, not opened). Remove "avoids every
   risk on sight" from the older card, but let it say it prefers safety.
8. **Phone reading costs a fifth of a standard deviation** (phone, age and language), *narrower*. The meta-analyses
   compare screens with paper. Their handheld estimate (g = −0.12) rests on 14 effects, most of them from students; it
   includes tablets and e-readers and is not significant (95% CI −0.27 to 0.03). The only comparison of phone and
   computer is Cotton et al. (−0.19 SD), where readers chose their own device.
9. **"Statistical realism" in Ji and Li** (AI stand-ins), *narrower*. "Realism" there is the mean absolute error of
   simulated attitude and behaviour scores against people's. It does not measure how human a text sounds. The ρ = 0.10
   is the country-level correlation between that error and the error in the intervention's effect. "Score catches,
   not how human it sounds" still follows, by analogy.
10. **Building personas from people's own words** (AI stand-ins), *narrower*. In Park et al. the "persona" baseline is
    a paragraph **the participants wrote about themselves**. A model's bullet summary of the interview scored 0.81,
    almost the interview's 0.83. The gain comes from the amount of true information about the person, not from who
    wrote the words. Only Li et al. tests model-invented detail. Neither study tests cards made of other people's forum
    posts.
11. **Model noise of 17 to 40%** (expert review), *narrower*. The range rises to 80% with Baymard's generic audit,
    where GPT-4 was given only "what UX improvements can be made?". Guerino's 24.3% is computed over all 111 raw issues,
    duplicates included; of the 68 distinct issues, 27 (40%) were false. The noise bar is realistic only for a reviewer
    given a task and a narrow brief.
12. **The left-digit failure** (replication), *narrower*. Fenneman et al. were not testing "single" choices: each
    person made about 18 repeated, paid decisions, with the price and both payoffs written on screen. The split between
    a glance and a deliberate comparison in the rewrite of instruction 10 is inference.
13. **"Run personas with the least thinking"** (AI stand-ins), *narrower*. In the same Hagendorff paper, GPT-4 with
    reasoning forbidden was still 88% correct and gave the intuitive wrong answer 10% of the time, against 55% for
    people. Turning thinking off does no harm, but it does not make the reader err the way a person does.

## What this means for the lab

- **The bar is harder than the expert report said.** Against what users met, one evaluator found about 4%, not a
  third. A first baseline far below 70% is expected. The case for scoring three merged runs and giving each case its
  task (the report's section 10) is stronger.
- **Keep textbook findings out of the reviewer.** The twins that showed the textbook default effect where the people
  did not are the clearest case for it.
- **For persona cards, real information beats who wrote the words.** Park's own summary agents show that a
  model-condensed real interview works. What hurts is invented detail (Li et al.). Cards should carry true facts about a
  kind of person, from the surveys, with no invented life story.
- **Treat the claim-against-actions check as an experiment, not as a known 45-to-1 cure.**
- **Fixes for the guide's owner, in addition to those in the reports.** In 3.3, 56% is right, not 42%. In instruction
  11, a large multi-year shekel sum does move people (return-chasing fell by about a third), while a small yearly one
  does not. In instruction 10, the split between a glance and a comparison is inference, not measured. In the older
  persona, "says it prefers safety" stays, but "reads described choices more cautiously" goes.
