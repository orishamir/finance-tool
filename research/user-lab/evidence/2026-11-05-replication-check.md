# Does what the behaviour guide says about people hold up?

Written 2026-11-05 for the user lab. It checks every finding in sections 1 to 3 of `behaviour-guide.md`, and the section 5
instructions built on them, against later evidence: meta-analyses, large re-tests, field data and critiques. The aim is
to stop the lab teaching the reviewer something false about people, and to stop it reciting textbook effects as if they
were large and universal.

**How to read the tables.** *Replication* opens with a verdict in bold: **holds**, **holds but smaller**, **depends on
context** (which context is said), **failed**, or **not known**. *Strength* uses the guide's scale (strong, moderate,
weak) for the finding as the reviewer should now use it. A source marked **(opened)** was read in full or in the
relevant part today and is filed in `research/library/` (its library id follows). A source marked **(not opened)** is
known from its abstract, a search excerpt or a secondary report only; its figures are as reported there and should be
checked before anyone quotes them. Those that matter are listed under "Blocked" at the end.

**The general picture, in four lines.**
1. The direction of almost every finding holds. What fails is the precision: several figures in the guide (20% of
   words, 57% above the fold, 2 points per 10 funds, 46% of comments about looks) come from one study, often old,
   often on people browsing without a goal, and are used as if they were constants.
2. Effects measured in papers are much larger than effects in the field. Nudges run at scale moved behaviour by about
   one sixth of what journal papers report; after correcting for publication bias, information nudges and nudges in
   the financial domain show no average effect at all.
3. Several effects depend on who the person is and what they are doing: banner blindness on having a task, choice
   overload on not knowing what you want, fees in shekels on the amount being large, overconfidence on the person.
4. The money findings that hold best are the ones about not computing: people do not minimise fees, chase past
   returns, and think of compound growth in straight lines. They hold in field data and representative samples.

## 8. The section 5 instructions: what should change

Only the instructions whose evidence changed are listed. The others (6, 13, 14, 15, 22, 23) stand as written; 22 and 23
rest on how the model behaves, not on evidence about people, and nothing here bears on them.

| # | Instruction now | What its evidence turned out to be | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Decide within the first screen; if nothing names your question, leave. | Leaving is front-loaded (holds), but measured on general browsing logs. People who come with a question and believe the page has the answer keep looking. | "Decide early whether this page is about your question. If the first screen does not name it, scroll a little looking for it; leave when nothing you have seen names it, and say where you gave up." |
| 2 | Most attention on two screens; below the third, headings and numbers only. | One desktop lab, 120 people; the share varies with page length and with whether the page looks finished. | Keep the shape, drop any number. Add: "If the page looks finished at the bottom of a screen, you assume it is." |
| 3 | Read about one word in five. | The 20% rests on 25 experienced volunteers' general browsing in 2004/05 and is a time-based upper bound, not a reading measurement. NN/g: how much a person reads depends on motivation, task and personality. | Replace the quota: "Read the words that look as if they answer your question, closely if the money matters to you; skim everything else by headings and numbers." Remove "about 20%" as a calibration target in section 6; measure instead whether the reader skips what does not bear on its question. |
| 4 | Start every line and block from the right. | Holds for reading direction within a line; whole-page Hebrew scanning is untested. | Keep, marked inferred. Do not let the reviewer rank a finding high only because something sits on the left. |
| 5 | Stop at the first plausible answer. | Holds; and people also split money across options rather than pick the best one. | Add: "If several options look reasonable, you may spread your choice or take the one marked as the pick, rather than work out which is cheapest." |
| 8 | Skip anything that looks like an ad, notice, disclaimer, source or "how we computed this". | People with a task look less at ad-like boxes and remember them poorly, but most do glance at them; the extension to sources and notes is inference. | "You notice boxes, small grey text and sources are there, glance at them, and rarely remember what they said. You read one only if it seems to answer your question or to warn you of a cost." |
| 9 | The first big number anchors you. | Anchoring is strong when you have no idea of the right value, moderate for prices, weaker for people who know. | Keep for personas with no idea of what a fee or a sum should be; weaker for the experienced investor persona. |
| 10 | Read numbers by their first digits; never add or check. | Left-digit effect is real in market data (car auctions, scanner data) and failed in one preregistered online test where payoffs were spelled out (repeated decisions, not single; corrected 2026-11-06). | 19 | A wide range or an unknown frightens you more than a known cost. | Rests on three model readers (guide 4.3), not on people. **One instruction the reviewer itself should carry, not a persona** (from section 6 of this report's tables): a small change
of wording, framing or order rarely changes what people do (nudges at scale: 1.4 points; information and finance
nudges: zero after bias correction). What changes what people do is structure (a default, a marked pick, what is
shown first) and what blocks them (not finding the answer, misreading a figure, a control that does not work, a word
they do not know). The reviewer should rank findings by how likely they are to block or mislead this person, not by
how many named biases they touch, and should not predict large behaviour changes from framing.

## What this means for a simulated person

Concrete behaviours, each with the evidence that now supports it.

1. **Leaves early when the page does not look like it is about their question, but looks a little before leaving if
   they came with that question.** Leaving peaks in the first seconds on 98.5% of pages (Liu et al.); half of all page
   visits last under 10 s (Weinreich et al.); but those are people browsing, and how much people read rises with
   motivation (NN/g 2020).
2. **Looks at the top and the start of lines first; attention falls steeply down the page.** Top-left regions first
   under every task (Buscher et al., 20 people, 361 pages); most viewing time in the first one or two screens on desktop
   (NN/g, 120 people). The precise share depends on the page.
3. **Scans; reads closely only the block that seems to answer their question.** Scanning is the default (NN/g eye
   tracking over 13 years); no study gives a fixed share of words.
4. **Glances at notices, sources and boxed notes and does not remember them.** Most people fixate ad-like content at
   least once but remember little (Hervet et al.); blindness is strongest on a task and on the side (Resnick and Albert).
5. **Does not pick the cheapest of near-identical options; spreads money or takes the marked one.** 0 to 6% put
   everything in the cheapest S&P 500 fund, 9 to 19% with a fee sheet (Choi et al.); in the field, a disclosure law
   moved about 7 points of money over two years (Kronlund et al.); defaults are the strongest nudge (d = 0.68; read 2026-11-06, `9295917b78d155bf`: 58 studies, 73,675 people,
   95% CI 0.53 to 0.83, 0.80 after trim-and-fill; stronger for consumer choices, weaker for environmental ones; a
   financial consequence did not significantly change it).
6. **Is pulled by past returns.** Choi et al. (lab); flows chase one-year returns more after disclosure (Kronlund et
   al., field); a returns line raised the fee chosen in Mexico (Hastings and Tejeda-Ashton).
7. **Thinks of growth in straight lines and is sure of it.** One in three fully linear, 96% underestimate, a chart did
   not help (Levy and Tasoff, representative US sample); 86% overconfident about their answers.
8. **Feels a shekel amount only when it is big next to their money.** Fees in currency help large investors, not small
   ones (Newall and Love, about 2,000 people); framing effects on the fee chosen were small (Hastings and Tejeda-Ashton).
9. **Judges a figure by its first digits at a glance, but reads it right when deliberately comparing two.** Field
   left-digit effects in 22 million car sales (Lacetera et al.); none in a preregistered online test with the payoffs
   written out (Fenneman et al.; 18 decisions each, so "deliberate" is the contrast, not "single", corrected 2026-11-06).
10. **Struggles with sliders and unfamiliar charts, more so with less education.** Sliders: 5% break-off against 1%,
    worse for the less educated (Funke et al.); a third of US and German adults have low graph literacy and low numeracy
    (Galesic and Garcia-Retamero).
11. **Trusts a tidy page more without saying so.** Appearance and credibility correlate 0.55 on finance pages, but
    people cite design in only 12 to 19% of their reasons (Kąkol et al.).
12. **Defers or takes the pick only when the options look alike and they do not know what they want.** Choice overload
    averages zero or a little above (two meta-analyses that disagree on the average; both read 2026-11-06) and appears
    under complexity, a hard task and unclear preferences.
13. **Is unsure and says so, unless they are the confident kind.** "Do not know" often hides partial knowledge
    (Bucher-Koenen et al.); better-than-average beliefs drive overconfident trading (Glaser and Weber; Barber et al.).
14. **Is not moved much by a reworded sentence.** Nudges at scale: 1.4 points against 8.7 in papers (DellaVigna and
    Linos); information and finance nudges near zero after correction (Maier et al.); an Israeli text-message nudge
    worked about half as well for Haredi and Arab parents (Haran Rosen and Sade: +12 points overall, "halved" for
    those groups; corrected 2026-11-06 from "less than half").
